Diablo II came out in 2000, and a quarter of a century of action RPGs have
mostly been footnotes to it. That is an irritating thing to say, because it
sounds like nostalgia, and nostalgia is usually what people mean when they say
old games were better.

So here is the argument system by system instead, including the parts that were
tedious then and are still tedious now.

## The loot loop

The thing modern ARPGs get wrong about Diablo II is that they copied the
dopamine and threw away the arithmetic.

Almost everything Diablo II drops is worthless. Not "slightly worse than what
you have" — actually worthless, vendor fodder, a white item you walk past
without a thought. The floor after a boss is mostly litter. That is not a
failure of generosity; it is the entire mechanism. A game where every drop is a
small upgrade has no drops at all, only a slow-moving number, and the player
stops looking at the floor because the floor has stopped saying anything.

Rarity in Diablo II is also structural rather than cosmetic. What can drop is
gated by the level of the area and the monster, which affix tiers are available
is gated by the item level, and the good affixes sit at the top of pools you
only reach deep in the game. So an item is not rare because a number said 0.1%.
It is rare because it required a specific thing to die in a specific place, and
then required the roll on top of that.

The consequence is that players remember individual items. Not builds — items.
That is the whole design succeeding.

## Rune words

The best crafting system the genre has produced, and the one almost nobody has
copied properly, because the part everyone copies is the recipe and the part
that matters is the scarcity.

Thirty-three runes, drop rates weighted so hard toward the low end that the
useful ones function as currency rather than loot. The high runes are not "hard
to get" in the way a modern grind is hard; they are rare enough that an economy
formed around them without anyone designing an economy. Prices in Diablo II
were quoted in runes because runes were the only thing stable enough to quote
prices in.

Then a rune word demands the runes *in order*, in a base item with exactly the
right number of sockets, of a type that can roll those sockets. Which means the
recipe alone is not the goal — you need the runes, and the base, and the base
has to be worth putting them in.

Enigma is the example everyone reaches for, and it earns it. Three high runes
in a body armour, and it gives Teleport to a class that could not teleport. One
item that changes how every non-sorceress character moves through the world
forever. That is not balance, and it is not an accident either. It is a system
willing to let a rare enough thing be genuinely, permanently transformative.

Modern crafting systems almost all fail the same way: they make the inputs
farmable. Once the input is farmable the output is a chore with a completion
date, and the economy is a spreadsheet.

## Skill trees that commit

Worth being accurate about this one, because the common claim is that Diablo II
has no respec and that is only true of the game as it originally shipped.

For a decade it genuinely had none. Patch 1.13, in 2010, added a limited one —
a single free respec per difficulty from Akara after clearing the Den of Evil,
and beyond that a Token of Absolution assembled from essences that drop off act
bosses in Hell. So respecs exist. They are just scarce, and the scarcity is the
design.

The effect is that a build is a position you take rather than a loadout you
swap. You put a point somewhere at level 12 and you live with it at level 80.
Synergies, added in 1.10, deepened this considerably: investing in a skill you
may never cast because it strengthens the one you do is a real decision with a
real cost, and it only means anything because you cannot undo it on a whim.

Compare a game that lets you reallocate everything at a click. Nothing is at
stake in any individual choice, so no individual choice is interesting, so the
tree becomes a menu you optimise once by reading someone else's answer.

Committing is not a limitation Diablo II failed to remove. It is the thing that
makes the tree worth having.

## Where it does not hold up

This is not a defence, and pretending the game is uniformly excellent would
undermine everything above it.

**The inventory is a chore.** Item sizes and a grid you play Tetris with, then
charms — which are permanent, take space, and mean the correct way to play is
with an inventory almost entirely full of things you never click. A cost you pay
constantly for a benefit you set up once.

**Mule characters.** Before shared storage the answer to running out of space
was a second character, held open in a second window, receiving items dropped on
the ground. An entire community habit built around a missing feature.
Resurrected fixed the storage; it is worth remembering it needed fixing.

**The trade economy lived outside the game.** Forum posts, spreadsheets, and
meeting strangers in a lobby. The runes were an economy; the game shipped no
tools for it whatsoever.

**Endgame repetition.** Baal runs and Chaos runs, over and over, because
efficiency-per-hour beat everything else and the game never offered a reason to
be anywhere else.

None of that is small. The point is that the systems underneath survived it —
the loot, the runes, the commitment — and those are the ones worth stealing.
Most games since have stolen the endgame loop instead.
