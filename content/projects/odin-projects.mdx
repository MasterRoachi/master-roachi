Twenty-four exercises from The Odin Project, a free course in making things for
the web, kept in one place rather than scattered across twenty-four. Almost
every one is online, so they can be tried rather than just read about.

## What the progression actually shows

The interesting part isn’t any single exercise. It’s the line they draw.

<Split src="/projects/odin-projects-a.webp" side="left">

The early ones are about laying out a page. **Odin Recipes** is the bare bones
of a web page and nothing else. The **Admin Dashboard** is dozens of panels
lined up until it stops being comfortable, and the **Landing Page** is the
same lesson another way. Nothing moves. The page is what you wrote.

</Split>

Then the page starts to move. **Etch-A-Sketch** builds its own grid while you
watch, and rebuilds it whenever you change the size — the first time the page
you wrote and the page on the screen stop being the same thing. **Coolculator**
is where it gets awkward: a calculator looks simple until you chain sums
together, and then the odd cases outnumber the normal ones.

**Tic-Tac-Toe**, **The Ark** and **KOI** keep what the program knows apart from
what the screen shows. The board lives in the program and gets drawn, rather
than the page being read back to find out whose turn it is. KOI builds its
whole site that way, with nothing written out by hand.

## Where it stops being exercises

**Testing Practice** is about proof: small programs with checks that show they
work, and the checks proved by breaking the code on purpose — the only way to
know a pass means anything. **Battleship** was then written checks first,
against a computer that hunts rather than guesses.

<Split src="/projects/odin-projects-b.webp" side="right">

Then the ideas underneath programming: a chain of items, a filing system that
finds anything at once (with a page to watch it grow), two ways of sorting the
same numbers raced against each other, and the shortest path for a chess
knight.

The race is the good bit. It is one thing to be told what happens when the
shape goes wrong, and another to watch the same numbers take ten times as long.

</Split>

Then the tools the professionals use: a CV that takes shape as you type and
prints clean, a memory game that reshuffles on every pick, and a shop with a
basket that remembers.

And then it leaves the page altogether. **portfolio=#** is this portfolio as a
real database you can ask questions of. **A server, by hand** is the machine
side of a website, written from scratch, with none of the shortcuts everyone
else uses.

That is the arc: from writing what you see, to writing what decides what you
see, to writing the machine on the other end.
