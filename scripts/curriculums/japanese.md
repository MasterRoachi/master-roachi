# Japanese — Learning Path

## Goal

Independent everyday Japanese — conversation, reading, writing — and then
onward without stopping. *Ad astra*, his word: there is no level at which this
curriculum declares itself finished.

The practical floor is the goal his own 2026 document set: hold a conversation,
read what is in front of him, write by hand. The ceiling is removed. Past the
point where a native newspaper is readable there is Classical Japanese, the
whole jōyō set, and the literature in `Aozora Bunko` — 17,700 works, free.

## What this replaces, and why

This is the second Japanese curriculum. The first was transcribed on 2026-08-17
from `Media/Courses/Languages/Japanese/Japanese-Language-Curriculum-2026-2029.md`
— a page map through fourteen books on the machine, with Pimsleur setting the
cadence at 90 sessions and the other thirteen placed alongside it. 118 lessons,
no material, and three problems that only show up when the aim becomes mastery
rather than a start.

**Pimsleur cannot be the spine.** It was chosen as the spine because it is the
one resource with a real rhythm — 90 lessons of about half an hour. But across
all three levels it teaches roughly 2,000 words, no reading, no writing and
little grammar. Every serious review says the same thing: excellent for
pronunciation and for speaking from day one, and *"unlikely to lead to fluency
on its own … recommended as a supplementary course to a more in-depth program
or textbook"*. The backbone of the curriculum was the one resource that cannot
carry it.

**RTK was two thirds of the curriculum and is removed**, at his word. Volumes
1–3 were 81 of the 118 sessions. The objections are specific rather than
matters of taste: the keywords are often not what the character means, the
characters are learned with nothing to connect them to, volume 2 teaches
readings for characters that were never seen inside a word, and the months it
takes are months of not learning Japanese. What RTK is genuinely good at —
telling similar characters apart, and writing them — is kept, as part of the
kanji track rather than as its own 81-lesson detour.

**The size was wrong by an order of magnitude.** Japanese is FSI Category IV:
about 2,200 classroom hours plus comparable self-study, and estimates for JLPT
N1 from zero run 2,200–4,500 hours. 118 sessions is perhaps 150–200 hours. A
curriculum to mastery is not a longer version of that one; it is a different
size of thing.

## Method: foundation first, with sprinkles of immersion

His choice, and it settles a real disagreement in the field.

The immersion-first camp (TheMoeWay, Tatsumoto) says: kana in a fortnight, a
fast grammar primer, then native material from almost the first week, mining
vocabulary out of whatever you are watching. Its milestones are content rather
than chapters — fifty series to stop being a beginner, one novel to leave the
beginner stage.

The foundation-first camp (Tofugu) says the opposite: *"when learning something
new, you should already know 80% of it"*. Kana, then several hundred kanji and
a thousand words, and only then grammar.

**Foundation first is the order here**, with immersion sprinkled through from
early rather than withheld until some gate. Concretely: comprehensible
listening from the first stage, graded reading as soon as kana is solid, native
material when it stops being decoding. Nothing waits for permission, and
nothing pretends that an hour of unintelligible anime is study.

Two other rulings shape everything:

- **Pitch accent from the start.** Tofugu puts it with the kana; the immersion
  guides treat it as a topic for later. Later means unlearning, so it is in
  from the first stage — and it is why the Sound strand exists at all.
- **Handwriting stays a goal.** Many adult learners drop it for typing. He
  keeps it, so stroke order is taught with every kanji set and composition by
  hand gets its own strand at the end.

## Every lesson teaches its point in full

The rule for the whole rewrite, and his: *"the lesson should teach the point in
full and then direct practice … the entire textbook doesn't need to be restated
but the material should be taught fully."*

So a lesson on な-adjectives explains な-adjectives — what they are, how they
attach, how they differ from い-adjectives, where the な disappears, with
examples written out — and then says what to do about it: the sentences to
write, the cards to add, what to listen for. It does not say "see Imabi 23".

This is what the sources are for and what they are not for. The research
decides the ORDER and the SUBSTANCE; the lesson carries the material. A source
on a lesson is attribution and somewhere to go deeper, never the place the
teaching lives — he does not want to open them to study.

Consequence worth writing down: **the lesson detail is the textbook**. At a few
hundred words a lesson this curriculum is a book, and that is the intended
outcome rather than an accident of scope.

## The resources

Chosen on merit, not on what is already on the machine — his instruction. Free
except where he already owns it, and nothing here needs buying.

### The spine

| Role | Resource | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grammar spine | **Imabi** (free, imabi.org) | 450+ lessons: Beginner 1–2, Intermediate 1–3, Advanced 1–3, Veteran I–II, and 36 lessons of Classical Japanese. The only free resource whose range reaches the top, which is the only kind that fits *ad astra*. His lean. |
| Functional and spoken | **Irodori** (free, Japan Foundation) | Starter, Elementary 1, Elementary 2, Pre-Intermediate — 18 lessons each, 72 in all, with PDFs, MP3 audio, word lists with audio and grammar worksheets. Built for *living* in Japan, which is his stated goal almost word for word, and it carries the speaking that Imabi does not. |

Imabi alone would be a grammar course with a reader bolted on; Irodori alone
stops around pre-intermediate. Together they are a spine and its spoken
counterpart, and both are free.

### The strands

| Strand | Resources |
| --- | --- |
| Script | Tofugu's kana mnemonic guides (free); his own *A Guide to Learning Hiragana & Katakana* for writing practice |
| Sound and pitch | **OJAD** and its prosody tutor **Suzuki-kun** (free) — 9,000 nouns, 3,500 declinables, ~42,300 conjugation patterns with actor audio, and whole-sentence prosody; **JAccent** (free) for lookups |
| Kanji | Frequency-ordered jōyō; **Jisho** and **WriteJapanese** (free) for stroke order animations across all 2,136; Hadamitzky *Japanese Kanji & Kana* (owned) as the reference |
| Vocabulary | Frequency-ordered core, then mining, both delivered to **the site's own flashcard app** (see below) |
| Listening | **Comprehensible Japanese** (Yuki) from absolute beginner; **Nihongo con Teppei** (1,400+ free episodes); Pimsleur I–III (owned) for shadowing |
| Reading | **Tadoku graded readers** (free, 200 PDFs across levels 0–5, CC licence, some with audio); **NHK News Web Easy** (free, daily, furigana toggle); **Aozora Bunko** (free, 17,700+ works); *Japanese Stories for Language Learners* (owned) |
| Speaking | Shadowing against Pimsleur and Irodori audio; **HelloTalk** / **Speaky** (free tiers) for exchange |
| Grammar reference | Makino, **Dictionaries of Basic, Intermediate and Advanced Japanese Grammar** (all three owned) — the standard references, and nothing free matches them |
| Lookup and mining | **Jisho**, **Yomitan** (free) — Yomitan is what makes reading native text tractable |

### Dropped, and why

- **Remembering the Kanji 1–3** — his call, for the reasons above.
- **Japanese From Zero! 1–5** — the old spine. Competent, but Irodori does the
  functional job better, has free audio, and does not run to five volumes.
- **Genki 1–2** — the standard classroom textbook, and it is on the machine, but
  it is built around a teacher and a class. Imabi plus Irodori covers the same
  ground with more range and better audio.
- **Essential Japanese Vocabulary** — the old plan had it open from JFZ 2
  onward. Usage questions are what the Makino dictionaries answer better.

Nothing dropped is deleted from the machine; it stops being on the route.

## Shape

**One ordered route.** No pacing, no dates, no gates — his ruling. The order is
the whole argument, and a lesson is done when it is done.

Lessons are drawn from the nine strands above and **interleaved**, because the
strands run concurrently in real study: pitch accent belongs beside the first
kana, not after it. A module is a stage of the route and holds whatever the
strands contribute at that point.

**The kanji track is its own run of modules**, placed in sequence rather than
dissolved into the stages — his word was "track". 2,136 characters in sets of
about eight, smaller at the start, each set taught through its commonest words
with readings in context and stroke order for the hand. Frequency-ordered: the
first 500 are about 80% of running text and the first 1,000 over 90%, so the
reading payoff front-loads and the tail is honestly a tail.

**Roughly 850–900 lessons.** That is the honest size of zero to mastery when
every lesson teaches its point.

**It is built a stage at a time**, and only what is written is in the database.
A lesson with a name and no material would be exactly the index this curriculum
exists to stop being, so the shape above is a plan rather than a set of empty
rows waiting to be filled. It is not a number to hit: stage 11 has no end,
and he can stop anywhere, since the route has no gates.

### A note on card kinds

Kana cards are `word` cards. A `kana` kind would be more honest — the prompt
for a kana is "read it", not "read it, then write it from memory" — but the
kinds live in a `CHECK` constraint, and widening one in SQLite means rebuilding
the table. That table holds every card's review history, and rebuilding it to
improve a label is not a trade worth making. `word` says "read it", which is
the right prompt; the writing practice is directed by the lesson.

## The flashcard app

His ruling: *"where anki is necessary, we must build our own flashcard app on
the site … i'm happy to do flashcards but i hate anki."*

Everything modern about Japanese assumes a spaced-repetition system running
daily, and the Vocabulary and Kanji strands are unworkable without one. So the
backend grows one, and it is a prerequisite rather than a nice-to-have — the
lessons that direct practice into it cannot be written honestly until it exists.

- **Algorithm: FSRS, via `ts-fsrs`.** Open source, actively maintained,
  TypeScript with ES module support, and it is the scheduler Anki itself moved
  to. So the scheduling is Anki's without Anki, in the language the site is
  already written in.
- **Cards come from lessons.** A kanji lesson's eight characters and a
  vocabulary lesson's batch are seeded as cards, so there is no separate
  deck-building chore. Mined cards are added by hand in the same place.
- **It is dashboard-owned data**, like habits: no build script, and a
  curriculum rebuild must not touch it. A card carrying three months of review
  history is not something a script can regenerate.

Spec and build are their own job, written up before it is started.

## Known decisions still open

- **Where the kanji track starts.** Kana must be solid first; whether it begins
  in stage 2 or stage 3 depends on how heavy stage 2 turns out.
- **How much Classical Japanese.** Imabi has 36 lessons and stage 10 allows 40.
  It is the one stage with no practical justification — it is there because the
  ceiling was removed.
- **Speaking before output is possible.** Shadowing works from day one; actual
  exchange with a person needs enough grammar to survive it, and the stage
  where that becomes true is a judgement call rather than a fact.
