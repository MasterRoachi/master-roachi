# Piano — Learning Path

## Goal

Read and play classical piano music. Literacy first: the ability to sit down
with a score you have never seen and work it out. Everything else — playing by
ear, chords, composing — stays available afterwards, and is harder to reach the
other way round.

## Where this came from

Unlike the Japanese path, this is not a transcription. There was no piano
material on the machine — no books in the library, nothing under Courses, and
`~/Media/Music` is empty — so this is a designed progression rather than a
document that already existed. Argue with it; every stage is a list in
`scripts/curriculums/piano.mjs`.

The repertoire order is a conventional one rather than an invention: Anna
Magdalena before Burgmüller before Clementi before Chopin is the sequence most
graded syllabuses converge on, for the good reason that each one needs
something the last one taught.

## Before starting

Two things this path assumes, neither of which is checked anywhere:

- **Weighted keys, 88 of them.** Technique built on unweighted keys does not
  transfer — the fingers never learn to produce a dynamic. 61 keys runs out
  inside stage 5.
- **Sheet music.** Everything named below is public domain and on IMSLP, so
  none of it needs buying. The exercise books (Hanon, Czerny Op. 599) are
  there too.

## How to use this

A stage is not a month. Move on when a milestone is genuinely solid — evenly,
at tempo, without stopping — and repeat one for as long as it takes. A
milestone half-done is worse than one not started, because it is practised
wrong.

Sight-reading appears in every stage from 2 onward rather than as its own
stage. It is the skill that decays fastest without constant use, and the
single thing most self-taught players neglect until it is the thing stopping
them.

## The stages

### 1 — At the keyboard

No reading yet. The hands learn where they are and what they feel like before
there is anything to decode.

### 2 — Reading

The grand staff, note values, and playing a written line one hand at a time
while counting out loud. Counting aloud is not ceremony: it is the only way to
catch a rhythm being guessed.

### 3 — Hands together

The real difficulty of the instrument, and the point most people stall. Two
independent lines, then dynamics and phrasing, which are what make it music
rather than correct notes.

### 4 — Scales and keys

All majors and minors, two octaves, hands together. Tedious and
non-negotiable: the fingering patterns learned here are what let you read an
unfamiliar piece in five flats without working out every note.

### 5 — Early repertoire

Real pieces, in the conventional order. Each one is chosen because it demands
exactly one new thing.

### 6 — Technique

Hanon and Czerny, the pedal, speed. Deliberately after stage 5 and not before:
exercises are only worth the time once there is repertoire they are obviously
serving.

### 7 — Intermediate repertoire

Bach's counterpoint, Chopin's hands, a Beethoven sonata movement. The point at
which "learning piano" becomes "playing piano".

### 8 — Keeping it

Ongoing, and never finished: sight-reading, aural work, transposition,
memorisation, recording yourself. This stage has no end and is the one that
makes the rest stick.
