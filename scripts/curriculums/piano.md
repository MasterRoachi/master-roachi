# Piano — Learning Path

## Goal

Play. Classical, anime openings, covers of songs he likes, and eventually his
own music. From nothing — he has never played and does not read a staff.

*Ad astra*, his word, as with Japanese: there is no level at which this
declares itself finished.

## What this replaces

The first version, designed on 2026-10-09: eight stages, 89 lessons, all
carrying material. It was a good document and this is a revision of it rather
than a replacement, because unlike Japanese it was never a transcription.

Four things change, and each came from either the research or from him.

**1. Notation AND chords, balanced — not literacy-first.** The old document
chose notation and argued that everything else stays available afterwards. His
ruling is "both, rather than either or". That is also what the material wants:
anime openings and pop covers are chord charts, classical is notation, and
composition needs both. The two strands run together from stage 2 and converge
in stage 7.

**2. Hanon is dropped**, at his word, and the reasoning is the same as RTK's in
Japanese. The objection is specific rather than a matter of taste: the patterns
do not occur in real music, so they are motor-control drills with no transfer,
and a widely-read free text calls them "essentially useless, or even harmful".
**Czerny stays and is not in question** — the criticism explicitly does not
apply to him, because his etudes are music.

**3. A body strand, from the first lesson.** The old document had no account of
rotation, arm weight or tension anywhere. For a self-taught adult with nobody
watching, that is the highest-stakes gap in it: tendinitis, carpal tunnel and
focal dystonia are what end piano, and they are all caused by things a teacher
would have corrected in week one.

**4. Theory becomes its own track**, running beside the route rather than
inside it — the same arrangement as Japanese's kanji, and now drawn as one on
the stages screen.

## The instrument

**An acoustic upright Hoffmann, about a century old, slightly out of tune.**
That is not a neutral fact and several lessons follow from it.

**What it gives.** A real action, real dynamics, and a real pedal from the
first lesson — all of which a digital piano approximates and none of which it
teaches properly. Technique built here transfers; technique built on
unweighted keys does not.

**What it costs.** No headphones, so practice has a time of day. No MIDI, so
none of the software that listens to what you played is available — no
real-time sight-reading feedback, no app that marks a wrong note. **The ear and
the metronome are the whole feedback loop**, which the curriculum has to take
seriously rather than assume away.

**And he does not record himself** — his ruling, asked directly. So no lesson
says to. Where the old document said "record it on a phone, the recording is
merciless", the new one has to find another way to notice the same thing, and
mostly that way is listening deliberately for one named fault at a time.

**It needs looking after.** Tuning drifts, an old action goes uneven, and
humidity matters. That is a lesson rather than an aside: a piano that is
unpleasant to play is a piano that gets played less.

## The strands

| Strand | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Body** | posture, rotation, arm weight, tension, when to stop |
| **Reading** | staff, rhythm, keys, and sight-reading daily from stage 2 |
| **Chords** | intervals, triads, inversions, progressions, lead sheets |
| **Technique** | scales, arpeggios, independence, speed, pedal, Czerny |
| **Classical** | the graded repertoire, in the conventional order |
| **The shelf** | anime, games and covers, folded in rather than kept apart |
| **Ear** | intervals, chords, melodies, and playing without the page |
| **Writing** | improvisation, then composition — the last strand to open |
| **Theory** | its own track, beside the route |

**Sight-reading appears in every stage from 2 onward** rather than as a stage
of its own. It is the skill that decays fastest without constant use and the
one most self-taught players neglect until it is the thing stopping them. The
research is unambiguous: ten to fifteen minutes a day on material **below** your
playing level, every day, separately from repertoire.

**Scales are spread, not blocked.** The old stage 4 was thirteen lessons of
scales in a row, which is the shape that gets abandoned. Each key is introduced
when a piece needs it, with one consolidation stage that joins them up. That is
his call delegated to me and this is the answer.

**No jazz**, his ruling. Chords and harmony are still needed — for covers, for
accompaniment and for writing — and they are taught through classical harmony
and pop progressions rather than through jazz voicings.

## How to use this

A stage is not a month. Move on when something is genuinely solid — evenly, at
tempo, without stopping — and repeat for as long as it takes. A piece half
learned is worse than one not started, because it is being practised wrong.

**Interleave.** The practice research has one finding worth building the
routine on: alternating between passages and skills produces markedly better
retention than drilling one thing to completion — **and it feels worse while
you do it**. Almost nobody does it for that reason. A curriculum can insist
where instinct will not.

**Practise slowly, with a named fault.** "Play it better" is not a goal.
"The left hand is late on beat three" is.

| — | Theory track, its own modules | ~40 | — |

**The written column is lessons in the database**, not an estimate. Where it
differs from the estimate the stage decided its own length: stage 1 needed
fewer than twenty because the body strand is short and specific, stage 4 needed
twenty-nine because the keys do not compress.

**Written a stage at a time**, and only what is written is in the database — a
lesson with a name and no material is an index entry, which is what this is
not.

## The cards

A piano deck is genuinely useful and it is mostly not note-reading.

**What goes in:** key signatures, chord spellings, intervals, Italian terms
and notation marks, scale fingerings, and theory definitions. All of it is
text, which matters because of the next paragraph.

**What does not: note-reading drills.** The lesson renderer has no music
notation and building one is disproportionate — and musictheory.net already
does exactly this drill, free, instantly, better than a deck could. Note
reading is directed there and the deck carries everything that is expressible
in words.

**The kinds are Japanese-shaped** — `kanji`, `word`, `grammar`, `sentence` —
because they live in a `CHECK` constraint and widening one in SQLite means
rebuilding the table that holds every card's review history. Piano uses `word`
for terms and `grammar` for anything with an answer to work out. The labels are
slightly wrong on screen and that is a smaller cost than the rebuild.

## The resources

Free, all of them, and chosen on merit.

| Role | Resource |
| --- | --- |
| Scores, classical | **IMSLP** — everything named here is public domain |
| Scores, the shelf | **MuseScore** — community arrangements at several difficulties |
| A structured course | **Hoffman Academy** — 400+ free video lessons |
| A second one | **Andrew Furmanczyk** — a complete free piano course and a free theory course |
| Theory | **musictheory.net** — free, complete, lessons and drills |
| Ear | **Teoria** and **Functional Ear Trainer** — the latter teaches notes by function |
| Chords and covers | **Bill Hilton** — the non-classical side, without the jazz |
| Classical tutorials | **Paul Barton** — 2,000+ videos |
| How to practise | **Fundamentals of Piano Practice** — free, and the source of the Hanon objection |
| Ordering, second opinion | **ABRSM** and **RCM** syllabuses — consulted, not followed |

**Difficulty on the shelf is a claim about an arrangement, not a piece.**
"Merry-Go-Round of Life" exists at grade 2 and at concert level. A stage
assumes the simpler arrangement; the hard one reappears later on purpose.
