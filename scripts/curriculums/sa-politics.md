# South African Politics — Learning Path

## Goal

Understand the country you live in well enough to follow an argument about it
and know which parts are contested.

Not to arrive at a position. The subject is one where almost every account is
written from inside a commitment, so the aim is to be able to tell what a
source is doing — which is a different skill from agreeing or disagreeing with
it.

## Where this came from

Designed, but the first five stages are backed by what is already in the
library:

| Source | Covers |
| --- | --- |
| Leonard Thompson, *A History of South Africa* (4th ed.) | the standard single-volume narrative, start to finish |
| *Cambridge History of South Africa* vol. 1 (Hamilton) | to 1885, pre-colonial and colonial |
| *Cambridge History of South Africa* vol. 2 (Ross) | 1885–1994 |
| Charles Feinstein, *An Economic History of South Africa* | conquest, discrimination, development — the material spine |
| Martin Evans, *The Boer War 1899–1902* | the war in detail |

**The library is strong to 1994 and thin after it.** Nothing on the Mbeki
years, state capture, the Zondo Commission or the present coalition. That gap
is filled with primary documents instead, which for the present is better
anyway:

- **The Constitution of 1996** — free, short, and the thing everyone argues
  about while rarely quoting it
- **The Zondo Commission reports** — free, enormous, and the most detailed
  account of how the state was actually captured
- **Statistics South Africa** releases — unemployment, inequality, the census

## How to read this subject

One rule, which is the whole reason stage 1 exists: **establish what a source
is before reading it for facts.** Thompson is a liberal historian; the
Cambridge volumes are academic consensus with a visible editorial line;
Feinstein is an economic historian who treats discrimination as a material
system; Afrikaner nationalist history and liberation history are both primary
sources about their own movements as much as accounts of events.

None of that makes any of them unusable. It makes them readable.

## The stages

1. **How to read it** — historiography first, because every later stage is
   reading somebody's argument.
2. **Before 1652** — the pre-colonial societies, and why "empty land" was a
   political claim rather than a fact.
3. **Conquest and colonies, 1652–1910** — Dutch and British rule, frontier
   wars, mineral revolution, the South African War, Union.
4. **Segregation to apartheid, 1910–1948** — the legal architecture built
   before the National Party, which is the part most accounts skip.
5. **The apartheid state, 1948–1990** — the laws, the economy, the resistance,
   the security state, and the collapse.
6. **The transition, 1990–1996** — negotiation, the interim constitution, the
   1994 election, and the final Constitution.
7. **The constitutional order** — what the document actually says, the three
   spheres, the Chapter 9 institutions, the courts.
8. **The present** — state capture and Zondo, the economy, the 2024 result and
   the coalition, and the live arguments.
