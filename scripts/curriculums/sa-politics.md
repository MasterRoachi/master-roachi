# South African Politics

Built by `scripts/curriculums/sa-politics.mjs`. **12 stages, 262 lessons, 654 cards,
872 lesson sources. Complete — every stage written, 14 October 2026.**

## Goal

Understand the country you live in well enough to follow an argument about it
and know which parts are contested.

**Not to arrive at a position.** The subject is one where almost every account
is written from inside a commitment, so the aim is to be able to tell what a
source is doing — which is a different skill from agreeing or disagreeing with
it. That is his ruling and it shapes every lesson: where a question is live,
the lesson gives the strongest form of each answer and says which evidence
would settle it, rather than settling it.

## What this rewrite changes

The first version (2026-10-09) was 8 stages and 32 lessons with about 560
characters of material each — the thinnest of the eight curriculums. It named
what to look up more than it taught it.

Four rulings, his, 2026-10-14:

1. **Expand the structure as well as the material.** The eight stages become
   twelve: the present gets stages of its own for the institutions, the
   economy, and the parties and the press, because that is where the free
   sources are strongest and where the arguments actually happen.
2. **As long as it needs.** No target. Each stage is sized to its material and
   the total is whatever that comes to.
3. **A card deck.** Unlike Godot. This subject is full of legislation, dates,
   institutions, constitutional sections and numbers that get argued about, and
   that is what a deck is for.
4. **The present is written as structures and arguments, with any specific
   result or office-holder in a dated snapshot.** The municipal elections are
   on **4 November 2026**, three weeks after this was written, so a curriculum
   that states the current composition of 257 councils as fact would be wrong
   within the month. See *The dated-snapshot convention* below.

And the standing rulings carry over: every lesson teaches its point in full and
then directs practice, so nothing requires opening a source to learn from;
no pacing, no dates, no gates; free resources only; tables throughout;
per-lesson sources.

## Two corrections to the first version

**The library holds eighteen books on this subject, not five.** The first
version's table named Thompson, both Cambridge volumes, Feinstein and Evans.
It also holds:

| Also in the library | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **Thula Simpson, *History of South Africa: From 1902 to the Present*** (OUP, 2022) | **a current academic narrative running to about 2021** — Mbeki, Zuma, state capture, Ramaphosa, July 2021 |
| **Hermann Giliomee, *The Afrikaners: Biography of a People*** (expanded ed.) | the major history of Afrikaner nationalism, and **itself a contested work** — catalogued as "Author Unknown" |
| **Matthew Blackman, *Rogues' Gallery*** | corruption from the VOC to the Guptas |
| **Timothy Stapleton, *A Military History of South Africa*** | Dutch–Khoi wars to the end of apartheid |
| **Ian Knight, *The Boer Wars* I (1836–1898) and II (1898–1902)** | the wars before the war |
| **Hofmeyr, *History of the Church in Southern Africa*** | the Dutch Reformed Church, which apartheid needed |
| **David Chidester, *Wild Religion*** | religion and the state after 1994 |

**So the claim that the library holds "nothing on the Mbeki years, state
capture, the Zondo Commission or the present coalition" was false.** Simpson
covers all four. The first version filled that supposed gap with primary
documents, which was the right instinct for the wrong reason — the documents
are still the better source for the present, and now they sit alongside a
narrative rather than in place of one.

**And one library book is not a source.** *The Unauthorised History of South
Africa* by "Stienie Dikderm" (Penguin Random House SA) is **comedy** — its
account of the Khoisan is a joke about the word *Bush*. It is excluded, and
named here so nobody wires it in later.

## How to read this subject

One rule, which is the whole reason stage 1 exists: **establish what a source
is before reading it for facts.**

| Source | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Thompson** | liberal historian; narrative and fair, with a visible view of what progress looks like |
| **Cambridge vols 1–2** | academic consensus, multi-author, an editorial line you can see by comparing chapters |
| **Feinstein** | economic historian; treats discrimination as a **material system producing measurable outcomes** rather than as attitudes |
| **Giliomee** | an Afrikaner liberal historian writing his own people's history — indispensable and committed, and the book itself is argued about |
| **Simpson** | recent, narrative, close to events; too near the present to have a settled reputation |
| **Blackman** | journalism as history, polemical by design, excellent on detail |
| **The TRC and Zondo reports** | **findings of a commission**, not of a court — a distinction stage 1 teaches |
| **Afrikaner nationalist and liberation history** | **primary sources about their own movements** as much as accounts of events |

None of that makes any of them unusable. It makes them readable.

## What "practice" means here

Every lesson ends by directing practice, as in every other curriculum. For this
subject practice is not a drill — it is one of six things:

| Kind of practice | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Read the document** | the section of the Constitution the lesson just explained |
| **Compare two accounts** | Thompson and Giliomee on the Great Trek; the TRC and Zondo on what a finding is |
| **Check the number** | take a figure from an argument and find it in StatsSA or the Budget Review |
| **Place a source** | read a page and say what it is doing before judging whether it is right |
| **Trace a thing to its origin** | a current property dispute back through 1913 |
| **Follow it live** | a bill through PMG, a judgment through the ConCourt collections |

**No lesson requires any of it to be understood.** The material teaches the
point in full; the practice is how it becomes yours.

## The dated-snapshot convention

Anything that can change with an election, a cabinet reshuffle or a court date
is written as a **snapshot**, marked with its date in bold inside the lesson:

> **As of 14 October 2026:** the ANC governs nationally in a coalition formed
> after the May 2024 election…

And the structural teaching around it does not depend on the snapshot being
current. So after 4 November 2026 the snapshots are wrong and the lessons are
still right, and the wrong parts are the ones in bold with a date on them.

**Stage 12 is written entirely as live arguments** — the strongest form of each
side and what would settle it — which ages far more slowly than any account of
who currently holds what.

**Stage 1 is first and is not optional.** It is the one stage whose absence
would make every other stage misleading, because a reader who cannot place a
source reads a committed account as a neutral one.

**Stages 8, 9 and 10 are the rewrite's real addition.** The first version
treated the present as a single stage of events. Events are stage 11; the
machinery that produces them is 8 to 10, and it is both more durable and more
useful.

## The deck

Cards, by his ruling. Two kinds, following Piano's precedent for a
non-language deck:

| Kind | For | Example front |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`word`** | an act, an institution, a term, a body, a date | *Natives Land Act, 1913* |
| **`grammar`** | a rule, a structure, a relationship | *How a section 76 bill passes* |

**What gets a card:** legislation that is still argued about, the Chapter 9
institutions, the constitutional sections people cite, the numbers that recur
(the Gini coefficient, the unemployment definitions, the debt-to-GDP ratio),
the commissions and what each was for, and the dates that are load-bearing.

**What does not:** anything contested. **A card asserts, and this curriculum's
whole posture is that the contested parts are not to be asserted.** So the
Mfecane gets no card about its cause; it gets a card about *what the debate is*.

## Standing decisions baked into the material

1. **Not to arrive at a position.** Where a question is live, both sides at
   their strongest, and what evidence would settle it.
2. **Place the source before using it.** Stage 1, and repeated wherever a
   lesson leans on one account.
3. **A commission is not a court.** The TRC made findings; Zondo made findings.
   Neither convicted anybody. The distinction is taught in stage 1 and held
   throughout, because collapsing it is the commonest error in public argument
   about both.
4. **Legislation is quoted, not paraphrased**, where the wording is the point —
   s25, s26, the 1913 Act's operative clause.
5. **Numbers carry their definition.** An unemployment figure is meaningless
   without saying which of the two definitions it uses, so every number in the
   material says.
6. **Snapshots are dated**, per ruling 4.
7. **No lesson requires a source to be understood.**

## Known thin spots

- **Nothing on the other Southern African states** except where they bear
  directly on South Africa — the border war, the frontline states, the migrant
  labour system. A regional history is a different curriculum.
- **Local government ended up as three stage-8 lessons** — the system, why it
  fails, and the provinces — **plus the devolution argument in stage 12**, which
  is more than planned and still less than it deserves. It is where most South
  Africans meet the state and the material on it is enormous; if it wants more
  it can have its own curriculum later.
- **No stage on language and culture politics** — the language clauses, Afrikaans
  at universities, the arts. Named inside other stages, not given room.
- **Pre-1652 is 14 lessons for several thousand years**, and the honest reason
  is the evidence: the archaeology is thin, the oral history is contested, and
  the stage's real job is to dismantle the "empty land" claim rather than to
  narrate.
- **The deck asserts, so it avoids the contested parts**, which means it covers
  the skeleton and not the argument. That is deliberate and it is a limitation.

## What the later stages ended up containing

Written 14 October 2026, after the four rulings. The first seven stages were
already written; stages 8 to 12 are the rewrite's addition and this is what
they turned into.

**8 · The institutions (22).** The stage's thesis is stated in its first
lesson and is the spine of everything after it: *South Africa's institutional
failures are overwhelmingly failures of capacity and of appointment, not of
design.* The evidence is the variance table — the ConCourt, the Reserve Bank,
Treasury, the AG, StatsSA and the IEC held; the NPA, the SOEs, the police, the
municipalities, the Public Protector and the Post Office did not. The stage
closes with the appointment correlation: **where a commission or a legislative
majority mediates the appointment, the institution mostly held; where one
person appoints, it mostly did not.** Zondo's recommendations are set against
what has actually been enacted.

**9 · The economy (22).** The minerals-energy complex as the organising idea,
with the strongest objection to it given (Australia, Canada and Chile are also
mineral economies without South Africa's labour market — so it was the labour
system, not the minerals). Then the labour market's eight explanations ranked
by evidential weight, with the grants-discourage-work claim named as the
weakest and most asserted. The redistribution paradox — the highest measured
market inequality and one of the most redistributive fiscal systems measured —
with the in-kind valuation objection given as the strongest technical
counter. Land and BEE get two lessons each, the law and then the evidence,
because conflating them is how both arguments go wrong.

**10 · Parties, movements and the press (20).** The seven-step recall chain is
the stage's central mechanism and it recurs in stages 11 and 12: majority →
closed lists → s47(3)(c) → an MP who defies the party loses the seat → the
s102 threat is credible → resignation is rational. Every party gets its own
account of itself, its measurable record and the strongest criticism made of
it. The press lesson maps the six owners and names Bell Pottinger as a
documented case of a narrative being commissioned and paid for.

**11 · From 1994 to now (26).** Longer than planned, because four subjects
needed their own lessons: **gender and gender-based violence** (which the plan
had not allocated and which the subject cannot do without), **the health
system and NHI**, **crime since 1994**, and **the SANDF and the regional
deployments**. The AIDS lesson holds both halves — the hundreds of thousands
of avoidable deaths and the world's largest treatment programme — because
stage 8's thesis has no better illustration. The state capture arithmetic
lesson establishes that the R500bn figure has no published method and that
recovery came almost entirely from foreign regulators and firms' own
settlements.

**12 · The live arguments (18).** Every lesson has the same five parts: state
the question precisely, state what is agreed, the strongest form of each side,
what evidence would settle it, and **what each side would have to concede**.
The last part is the one that makes the stage work. Three findings recur:
several arguments are much narrower than the public version (NHI is about
sequencing, not about universal coverage); several have an available test that
nobody has run (the class-based BEE pilot, the SRD labour-supply analysis, a
devolved commuter rail corridor); and two — electoral reform and the
constructive vote of no confidence — need only ordinary legislation and have
not been done because every party in Parliament benefits from the status quo.

**The deck.** 654 cards across 229 lessons, `word` and `grammar` only, per
Piano's precedent. Stage 12's cards assert only what is *agreed* or what the
*test* is, never a side of a live argument — which is the standing ruling
applied to the deck.
