# Modern Greek — Learning Path

## Goal

**The liturgy, and the Greek people at church** — his answer, in that order, and
it decides the whole shape of this document.

The second half of it is what Modern Greek is for: talking to people, being
talked to, following a conversation after the service, reading what they read.
The first half is mostly **not** Modern Greek, and saying so plainly is the
honest start.

*Ad astra*, his word, as with Japanese and Piano: no level at which this
declares itself finished.

## What this does not contain, and where it goes instead

**The liturgical language is a different language.** The Divine Liturgy is in
Koine and Byzantine Greek, the hymnography is in a learned register older than
that, and the readings are from the Septuagint and the New Testament. A fluent
speaker of Modern Greek follows some of it, guesses at more, and misses a great
deal — and that is normal rather than a failure of their Greek.

**So it is deferred, by his ruling, to its own curriculum**: classical Greek
moving into theological and liturgical Greek. That curriculum is where
katharevousa, the learned register, the Koine verb system, the hymn texts and
the service books belong.

**What this curriculum gives towards it** is still substantial: the alphabet,
the sound system, several thousand words with their classical ancestry visible,
the case system, and enough grammar that the later curriculum starts from
something rather than nothing.

**The learned register is skipped here** — also his ruling. Where a learned
form is unavoidable in modern use (a sign, a church notice, a headline) the
lesson says what it is and moves on.

## What this replaces

The first Greek curriculum, built 2026-10-09: 98 lessons transcribed from the
FSI *Greek Basic Course* and Pimsleur, with **no material in the lessons at
all**, on the old rule that a transcribed curriculum keeps its substance in its
resource. That rule is overturned — every lesson here teaches its point in
full — so this is a rewrite rather than a revision.

**Four decisions, all his:**

**1. FSI is demoted.** It is thorough, it is free, he owns it with audio for
every unit, and it is from the 1960s: drachmas, the monarchy, katharevousa as a
living formal register, and a drill style that assumes a classroom and an
instructor. It stays as a **drill and audio resource** — the pattern practice is
genuinely good — and stops being the spine.

**2. Pimsleur stays.** Sixty lessons, owned, half an hour each, and the best
thing available for getting the mouth working before the eye takes over.

**3. Script from lesson one.** Twenty-four letters, learnable in a week, and
romanised Greek costs months later. Handwriting is taught too, by his ruling.

**4. Spelling is drilled** — "drills bruh" — which is why orthography is a
track rather than a remark. It is the main memory burden in Modern Greek and
nothing else in the language rewards drilling as much.

## The instrument of the language: five difficulties

Everything in the stage order below is arranged around these.

**1. Aspect.** Nearly every verb has two stems — imperfective and perfective —
and the choice between them is compulsory in the past, the future and the
subjunctive. There is no infinitive. This is the single hardest thing in the
language for an English speaker and it gets a stage of its own.

**2. The case system.** Four cases, three genders, several declensions, and the
article inflects with the noun. It is smaller than German's and bigger than
anything English has.

**3. The orthography.** Five vowel sounds, and *ι η υ ει οι υι* all say *i*;
*ε* and *αι* both say *e*; *ο* and *ω* both say *o*. Which spelling a word takes
is a fact about that word, inherited from Ancient Greek. Hence the track.

**4. The stress accent.** Written, compulsory, and meaningful — *πότε* and
*ποτέ* are different words. It also moves when a word inflects, by rules.

**5. Clitics and word order.** The weak pronouns attach to the verb in a fixed
order and move depending on the mood; word order is freer than English and the
freedom carries meaning.

## How to use this

**Speaking matters** — his ruling, as it was for Japanese. Every lesson's
examples are read aloud, at speaking speed, not scanned. Greek is not a
language you can learn to read silently and then suddenly speak.

**The Ellinomatheia levels are the skeleton**, also his ruling: A1 through C2,
as published by the Centre for the Greek Language, whose past papers and audio
are free. **No exam is taken.** The levels are used because they are an honest
external description of what "knowing some Greek" means, and because the free
material is graded against them.

**No pacing, no dates, no gates.** An ordered route and nothing else.

**Stage 4 absorbed the past tenses** (rewritten 2026-10-14). The plan had them
in stage 6, and aspect cannot be learned without them: the aorist against the
imperfect is where the distinction is most visible, and teaching the future and
the subjunctive without it would mean teaching the choice twice. Stage 6 now
takes what is built on top — the perfect tenses, the pluperfect, the
conditional, the passive across the tenses, and the participles.

**Comparable to Japanese in length**, at his word — that came out at 382
lessons, and this is planned at roughly the same.

**Written a stage at a time**, and only what is written is in the database. A
lesson with a name and no material is an index entry, which is what this is not.

## The two tracks

Drawn as their own row of cards beneath the stage deck, like Japanese's kanji
and Piano's theory, and **scheduled** rather than left for him to guess at —
"How this curriculum works" carries the pairing table.

**Spelling 1–5**, from stage 2 onward: the *i* spellings, the *e* and *o*
spellings, the double consonants, final *-ν*, the accent rules in full,
diaeresis, punctuation, abbreviations, numerals, handwriting joins, and the
spelling of the endings — which is where most of the leverage is, because
endings recur.

**Forms 1–5**, from stage 3 onward: the noun declensions as tables, adjectives,
the article, pronouns in full, the verb conjugations, the aspect pairs of the
common verbs, irregular verbs, participles and the passive. Reference material,
drilled against the deck rather than read once.

## The cards

**Deck per curriculum**, as everywhere. Open-ended vocabulary, his ruling —
there is no target number and no "core 2,000".

**What goes in:** words with their gender and plural; verbs as **aspect pairs**
(γράφω / γράψω), because a verb without its perfective stem is half a verb;
spelling cards, where the question is which *i* a word takes; the article and
declension patterns; set phrases; and the church and parish vocabulary.

**Kinds are Japanese-shaped** — `kanji`, `word`, `grammar`, `sentence` — because
they live in a `CHECK` constraint. Greek uses `word` for vocabulary, `grammar`
for anything with a form to work out, `sentence` for whole phrases. The labels
are slightly wrong on screen and that is cheaper than rebuilding the table.
