# Philosophy — the plan

**Status: complete, 2026-11-04. 14 stages, 375 lessons, 1,219 cards, 896 lesson
sources, all applied.** Written in parallel from one brief and merged by the
same checks as History. Every quotation was fetched from a public-domain
translation and checked word for word; 20th-century texts are paraphrased and
say so.

His rulings: **the Western philosophical canon**, **from zero**, **running to
the present**, and **any free resource**, meaning free online or a printed book. Built the way History was: one plan, the stages written from one brief,
merged and checked, then applied.

## Goal

Know what the great philosophers argued, well enough to state each argument in
its strongest form, give the best objection to it, and say where it sits in the
long conversation. Then be able to do some of it yourself.

**Not to arrive at a position**, his standing rule on contested subjects.
Philosophy is the contested subject. A lesson states an argument as its best
defender would, gives the strongest objections and replies, and leaves the
verdict open, saying plainly where there is broad agreement that an argument
fails.

**Christian philosophers are read here as philosophers**: Augustine, Boethius,
Anselm, Aquinas, Palamas, Pascal, Kierkegaard. What they argued, and whether it
holds as argument. Their theology belongs to the Theology curriculum, which
points across.

## How it is read

- **Each stage opens with a context lesson** ("Context: …"): the world the
  thinkers lived in, and the questions it pressed on them.
- **Each stage has one reading lesson** ("Reading: …"): a short passage from a
  public-domain translation, read closely, line by line. Twentieth-century
  texts are mostly in copyright, so their readings paraphrase and say so.
- **Each stage closes with a "Looking back" lesson**: what was settled, what
  was left open, and what the next stage picks up.
- **Arguments are set out**, as premises and conclusion where that helps, with
  the main objections and replies. Terms are defined the first time they appear.

## The resources

**Free online:**
- The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* and the *Internet Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy*, for every figure and problem.
- Jonathan Bennett's *Early Modern Texts*: modernised Descartes to Kant, free.
- Public-domain translations: Jowett's Plato, the Oxford Aristotle (Internet
  Classics Archive), Long's Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus, Pusey's Augustine,
  and Kant, Hume and Mill on Project Gutenberg.
- *forall x* (open textbook) and Richard Zach's *Boxes and Diamonds* for logic.
- MIT OpenCourseWare 24.01, *Classics of Western Philosophy*, for the shape.

**Printed books** (depth): Kenny's four-volume *New History of Western
Philosophy*; the Complete Plato and the Complete Aristotle; Hume's and Kant's
complete works; Plotinus's *Enneads*; Boethius; Augustine's *City of God*;
Copi, Hurley, Klenk and MacFarlane on logic; Russell's *Problems of
Philosophy*; Hadot's *Philosophy as a Way of Life*; Feser on Aristotle,
Aquinas, Locke and mind; Loux on metaphysics; Lycan on language; Cover on
science; the Cambridge Companions to Hume, Kant and Kierkegaard.

## Cards

`word` cards for terms, people and works, each with a date ("*Meditations on
First Philosophy* — Descartes, 1641"); `grammar` cards that ask for an argument
or an objection, with the answer set out ("State Zeno's Achilles paradox").
About 3 to 4 per lesson.
