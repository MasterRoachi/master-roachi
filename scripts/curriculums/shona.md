# Shona — the plan

**Status: approved 2026-10-19; written in full 2026-11-05. Twelve stages,
280 lessons, 1,239 cards: Stages 1–10 follow FSI's Units 1–49 to the end of
the course, Stage 11 reads the Shona Bible, and Stage 12 is Ad astra. The
proverbs and respect stage the plan named is not written: see Open.**

His rulings so far: Shona is the first of the five empty curriculums to be
built; **from zero**, zero to mastery, like Japanese and Greek; and the same
three phases as every rewrite — research, a plan he approves, then the
writing.

## What Shona is, and which Shona

chiShona is a Bantu language of Zimbabwe, spoken by roughly 10–15 million
people as a first language. **Standard Shona** is the written standard, based
mainly on the **Zezuru** dialect of the Harare area, and it is this
curriculum's Shona. **His ruling: Standard Shona only, with the other dialects
(Karanga, Korekore, Manyika, Ndau) named where they come up rather than
taught.**

**Correction, found while writing Stage 1.** The plan first said both free
courses teach Zezuru-based Shona. FSI does not: its foreword says it is "based
on the speech of two individuals, representing **Manyika** varieties of Shona,
but with certain systematic emendations in the direction of the more central
dialects." So FSI's recordings are an eastern voice. Carter's course is the
Zezuru one, and is where the standard forms come from; every lesson says
which of the two a form is.

What makes it hard for an English speaker, and so what the plan is built
around:

- **Noun classes.** Every noun belongs to a class, marked by a prefix, and
  that class is echoed on the adjectives, possessives, demonstratives, numbers
  and verbs that agree with it — the concords. It is the grammar of the
  language, not a chapter of it, so it is a strand that runs the whole length.
- **Tone.** Shona is tonal — high and low — and standard spelling does not
  mark it. A word learned without its tone is learned half. Like pitch accent
  in Japanese, **tone is in from the first stage**, learned by ear from the
  audio and from tone-marked texts, not left for later.
- **The verb.** One verb carries subject and object, tense, negation, mood and
  a chain of extensions (causative, applicative, passive, reciprocal) as
  affixes on one word. Long words are built, not memorised.
- **The sounds.** Mostly friendly to English, with a few that are not: the
  "whistled" sibilants written **sv** and **zv**, implosive **b** and **d**,
  and the breathy-voiced consonants.

**The rule for writing it.** Japanese and Greek were written largely from
knowledge; Shona will not be. Every grammar point, concord table, tone pattern
and word in a lesson is taken from the FSI and Wisconsin texts — the same rule
the kanji track kept — because a concord learned wrong is reviewed wrong for
months, and Claude's Shona is thinner than its Japanese. Where the two courses
disagree, the lesson says so.

## The strands

As in Japanese, strands run side by side through each stage rather than one
after another.

- **Sound** — tone above all, then the consonants English lacks.
- **Noun classes and concords** — the classes one pair at a time, each with
  the full set of agreements it triggers.
- **The verb** — concords, tenses and aspects, negation, mood, then the
  extensions.
- **Vocabulary** — cards from every lesson, through the site's own flashcards.
- **Listening and speaking** — FSI audio and shadowing from day one; real
  speech once the drills stop being decoding.
- **Reading** — tone-marked course texts first, then the Shona Bible, proverbs
  (tsumo), and modern prose.
- **Culture** — greetings and respect (the plural of respect is grammar, not
  manners), totems (mitupo), and the Zimbabwe the language lives in.

## How a lesson is written

Built from the page images of FSI and Carter, not their OCR text, which
mangles the tone marks (FSI's *zvavó* comes out as "zvav6"). Every dialogue
line was re-read at high resolution before it became a card. Where the two
courses describe a thing differently — the whistled *zv*, for one — the lesson
gives both and sends him to the recording.

FSI writes two tone systems at once, central and eastern, with *ǎ* and *â*.
The lessons say the **central** reading (*ǎ* low, *â* high), because the
standard is central, and say so in every lesson that uses the marks.

**Stage 1** draws on FSI Units 1–4 and Carter's Lesson I: the sounds (stress,
tone, the w-sounds, *v*, *zv*/*sv*, the voiced *h*), how both courses mark
tone, the morning and midday greetings in both forms, the tone rules met in
them, the subject prefixes of the past, concord, the plural of respect, the
noun-class table, the pro-verbs, leaving and courtesies, and a closing lesson
of real greetings with his partner.

**The stages follow FSI's units, not the first guess.** The plan first had
Stage 2 as "the first noun classes, and 'to be'"; FSI's Units 5–8 teach the
tenses instead (-no-, -ka-, -cha-, -nga-), with places, numbers, possessives
and strong adjectives beside them, so Stage 2 follows FSI and the later rows
are retitled to match. Their titles past Stage 3 stay rough until the units
are read.

**Stage 2** draws on FSI Units 5–8: meeting and asking who, where and what
work; the -no- tense with every class's subject prefix and its tones;
locatives and where-questions; the -ka- tense, days, numbers and their
concords, periods and "ago"; -ndo- and -zo-; the -cha- tense, one-syllable
verbs and possessives of both kinds; the store and the -nga- tense; the
pronouns, places to go, the strong adjectives and direct-only reported speech.

**Stage 3** draws on FSI Units 9–12: buying at a stall; *-rí* "be", alone and
with an infinitive for "am doing"; the "what?" ending *-nyi*; the
demonstratives of every class; the past of today (the greetings' tense, at
last explained) and the *-i-* "used to"; relative verbs, made by tone alone,
and with them most English adjectives and the *ndí-* "it is" forms; and the
negative of the *-no-* tense, with *-gona*, *-dá* and *-zívá*. FSI's 1965
coinage is named as history and not carded.

**Stage 4** draws on FSI Units 13–16: classroom Shona; the past negative
(*handíná ku-*); commands, singular and plural; *-zve* "again"; *na* "and,
with" and its vowels; *-ne* "have"; object prefixes for persons and classes,
with their tones; the independent form, which makes a noun or adjective a
sentence; and the *-o-* "about to". FSI's **ˣ** — tone that varies between
speakers but not east from west — is written after its syllable and left to
the speaking partner.

**Stage 5** draws on FSI Units 17–20: the hospital dialogue and directions;
the place classes 16, 17 and 18 (*pa-*, *ku-*, *mu-*) as classes, with a place
as subject and *pane/kune/mune* "there is"; *kwa-* for someone's place
(*kwedu*, *kwaani?*); the aspect prefix *-mbo-* "just, ever"; ordinals with
*-chi-*; a meal and the kitchen; the enclitic *-wo* "too"; *na* with the
personal and the class pronouns; *~ose* "all"; Unit 18's drills setting every
tense beside its negative (with the new *ha-* + *-ri* + infinitive); the
linking prefix "of"; sentences with no verb; cooking; and *-dini* again. The
plan's "the tenses' negatives" is only those drills — the units teach no new
negative tense, so the title follows what they do teach. Two lines of the
Unit 19 dialogue are given untoned: their stacked marks do not print clearly
enough to copy. FSI spells Stage 4's *muriwo* as *murivo* in Unit 20; the
lesson says they are one word.

**Stage 6** draws on FSI Units 21–26: buying at the store, and the applicative *-ir-/-er-* "for someone" (*-gadzirira*, *-vadzira*, *-dzidzira* "study"); the prefix *nge-/nde-* "with, by, about", on its own and before demonstratives; a noun joined to an infinitive by *-o-* (*demo rokutema miti*, "an axe for cutting trees"); *-regera*, *-fanira* "must", *-natsa* and *-ramba* before an infinitive; peoples and dialects (*muZezuru*, *muManyika*, *muNdau*), and *-fanana*/*-siyana* for alike and different; the reflexive *-zvi-* and *~oga* "alone"; *sa-* "like"; kinship terms and Class 1 against Class 1a; the passive *-w-* (*-iw-* on one-syllable stems) with *na-/no-/ne-* for the doer, including passives English lacks (*Kudhibhi kwaendwa nababa*); the *-chi-* participial after *kuti* "if" and after *ngenyi* "why"; the neuter *-ik-/-ek-* "-able", set against the passive; the *-aya* tense of *-va* "now" (*Mvura yaya kupisa*, *Vayana makore mashanu*); *-ramba* "go on" with the *-chi-* form and "refuse" with an infinitive; *-bvira* "really"; and Unit 26's three *~o* forms: *-na-* + concord + *-o* "have it" (*tinacho*), *nde-* with possessives "mine, yours" (*ndevangu*), and *-po/-ko/-mwo* "there" after verbs. Transport, farm animals and newspaper words come with them. FSI leaves most practice conversations, and the second half of several drills, untoned; the lessons print them as FSI does and leave the tones to the speaking partner.

**Stage 7** draws on FSI Units 27–31: planting flowers and maize, cooking sadza and *muriwo* step by step; the tonal participial ("when I've finished", *ndãpédza*; "him being at home", *ãri*), with FSI's new mark *ã*, its first syllable high; participials of *-na* (*mũiné*, "keep to the left"); *chaipo* "itself" for every class, and *chaizvo*; the negative participial *-singa-* … *-i* ("without stopping") and *-sina* ("without", "not having"); negative relatives, spelled like the participials but low-high in tone; asking the way to Muradzikwa and Nyanga; a trip to America, with *nge-* "by, about" and the verbs that mean "become tall, bad, many"; possessives with two concords (*zvivako zvaro*, "its buildings"); independent nouns (*Múgwágwa*, "it is a road") in full; the reciprocal *-an-*; the subjunctive (*tíéndè*) and the hortative (*ngátíéndè*, "let's go"), with *-to-* "already" beside it; "the field that they ploughed" — concord + linking prefix + participial (*munda wavakarima*), and Manyika's demonstrative instead (*apo tãvamba*); the headman's council, with *sá-* in titles (*sabhuku*); and FSI's own word that from Unit 31 its tone marks thin out and the student supplies them. Lines where FSI stacks two marks on one syllable are given with that syllable unmarked, and every slip in FSI's drills is named where it falls.

**Stage 8** draws on FSI Units 32–37: a wedding invitation and the house made ready for a guest; nouns for the doer (*muimbi*, *mubiki*) and, from Unit 37, for the work (*ubiki*, *urimi*); the negative command *usa-/musa-* and the negative subjunctive *tisa-* with and without an object; the causative pairs (*-pera/-pedza*, *-rara/-radzika*); *-sati* "not yet" with the participial of the *-a-* tense and FSI's tilde for dialect tone; bathing a baby, *-dai* "like, for example", and *-na-* "must" beside *-kafanira*; travel by bus; commands with an object (*Ípe*, *Rídye*, *Ríisé*), a class 16 relative as a place (*pàkáǒmǎ*), and a command with a subjunctive of purpose; minding children, the diminutive classes 12 *ka-* and 13 *tu-*, the enclitic *-su*, *chi-* before a command, and *-natsa* "do well" with its contraction; *-chi(ri)* and *-ndo-* "still"; the verb phrase with *-nga* (*vakanga vasvika*, *vanozonga vasvika*), in all three moods; *kunyanya* after a verb; the conditional with *dai*; *nde-* "it is X's" and *cho-* + infinitive "something to do"; and *ka-* for how many times. These units leave more and more to the student — Unit 37's dialogue is "raw materials", and many drills have no tones and no English — so where a lesson's English is built from FSI's word lists rather than given by FSI, it says so, and FSI's repeated instruction to take the tones from the tutor is passed to the speaking partner. Unit 34 is dialogue only, as FSI's contents say; its drills (*Ípe*, *pàkáǒmǎ*, the purpose subjunctive) are taught from its exercises.

**Stage 9** draws on FSI Units 38–43 (PDF pages 385–443): Unit 38's eight short dialogues (the post office, the car tax, selling potatoes, a visit to the sick with the totem greetings); *zv-* with no noun ("adverbial"); the demonstrative *-ya*, "the one we both know", and *zvíyá* "by the way"; the ideophonic form after *-ti*; the dependent *-ka-* tense "if, when", its negative *-kasa-*, and *-kange* with a participial; then Unit 39's texts — the four seasons of Mashonaland, a farmer, the classroom, greeting travellers, moving house — with *-mwe* "some, other" beside *-mwe* "one", *zvimwe* "perhaps", *-cha-* as "still", nouns made from verbs, *-ti* with a participial beside *-ti* with an ideophone, the linking prefix before an infinitive or a participial, and *-chí* "still" beside *-áẏá* "now"; Unit 40's two dialogues and fifty two-line fragments, split across two lessons; and the narrative texts of Units 41–43 (Tsuro and Diro twice, Hyena, Chuma and Chibharo, the kudu hunt, the forgotten axe), each read FSI's way — tones marked with the partner, versions A to D, the questions, the retelling. From Unit 40 on FSI prints its texts untoned and without English: the lessons give the Shona as printed, glosses that say they are ours and are built only from words FSI glosses, and name every word FSI never glosses (*manyana*, *karekare*, *imbwa*, *chimbudzi*, *mhaka*, *nyanza*, *ngowání*, *-donha*); their cards carry no reading. Twenty-nine lessons, 141 cards.

**Stage 10** draws on FSI Units 44–49 (PDF pages 444–498; the Glossary begins on 499), the last of FSI's basic course: no dialogues, no notes and no drills, but texts printed four times over (A to be toned, B read clean, C without prefixes, D without roots), four questions on every sentence and short word lists, with FSI's seven steps for using them from the head of Unit 41. It reads every text in full: three tales (the monkey and the gourd; the Hare, the well and Diro; the Hyena, the Hare and the calves), Southern Rhodesia's riches and borders in 1965, the politics and newspapers of that year, women's clubs, a coughing disease, and totems. From them it draws out the grammar that the texts use and Stages 6–9 taught: FSI's tilde, the only tone mark, for tonal participials; *-sina* and *-sika-*; *nge-*; the passive and its doer; class 12 *ka-* for the Hare and *ka-* for "times"; the subjunctive and *nga-*; *-chi-* and *-chiti* "saying"; *-kange* + participle; *-ava* and *-aa-*; the story's *-ka-* chain and joining words; *kudai* "such as"; *~mwe na~mwe* "each and every"; the compass; and FSI's four questions. FSI gives no English for these texts, so every sentence gloss is the lesson's own, built from FSI's word lists and Glossary, and says so; the texts are untoned in FSI, so sentence cards carry no reading, and single words take the Glossary's tones where it marks them. There are no FSI recordings for these units. FSI's Manyika *w* for *v* (*wamba*, *kuwa*, *nguwa*) gets a lesson, and the colonial names are given as history.

**Stage 11** draws on the Shona Bible and FSI's glossary: twenty-three passages an Orthodox reader meets in prayer and the services, from short to long — Genesis 1:1–5 (the lesson that introduces Biblica's text, its licence, its lack of tone and its standard, central voice beside FSI's eastern one), the Lord's Prayer, the publican, the Song of Simeon, the Beatitudes, Psalm 51 (50) and Psalm 103 (102) in two halves each, the Annunciation, the Magnificat, the Nativity and the shepherds, John 1:1–14, Philippians 2:5–11, the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son in two halves, 1 Corinthians 13, the myrrh-bearers (Matthew 28:1–10), Thomas (John 20:19–31) and Emmaus in two halves — and a closing lesson that reads Psalm 23 (22) and Matthew 11:28–30 unseen. Every Shona line is Biblica's, untouched, set verse by verse beside the BSB; every gloss is FSI's glossary or the BSB verse, and the lessons say which, and where an analysis is the lesson's own. The text is untoned, so tone is given only for words in FSI's glossary, read from its page images (*kwázíwáí*, *-kúdzá*, *mambákwedzá* …), and the speaking partner supplies the rest. Psalms carry both numbers, the Hebrew that both texts use and the Septuagint's. The grammar named is the course's: Stages 1–5 by lesson name, and FSI's later units by its own terms — the hortative and subjunctive (Unit 30), passive and *-chi-* participial (Unit 23), applicative (21), reflexive (22), neuter *-ik-* (24), negative relatives and *-na* participials (28), reciprocal (29), negative commands (32), *-dai* (33, 37), *-chiri* (36), *ka-* of frequency (37), *-ya* and the dependent *-ka-* (38). Where Biblica differs from FSI the lesson says so: the linking *o* (*nokuti*, *rokutanga*, never *nekuti*), *sei* for FSI's *sényi*, *meso*, *chiso*, *mikova*, *-gamuchira*, *-teerera*, *-udza* beside FSI's *mésó*, *usú*, *musúwo*, *-gashira*, *-tèrera*, *-údzírá*; and words the Bible uses in a sense FSI does not give (*denga* "sky" → heaven, *nyama* "meat" → flesh, *mhandara* "girl" → virgin, *chando* "cold" → snow, *-dzivirira* "prevent" → bear). Two verses where the BSB follows a different Greek reading from Biblica (Luke 1:37, 1 Corinthians 13:3) are named in their lessons and not carded. The Paschal greeting is deliberately not supplied: the lessons send him to his partner or parish for it.

**Stage 12**, Ad astra, has three lessons and no cards: what comes after the course, a shelf of Shona to read and hear, and keeping it over years. It names no Shona form the course has not taught.

**Every stage names FSI's slips where they fall** — misprints, English that does not match the Shona, a drill line that repeats the one before — and gives the right form where it is clear; each stage's writer listed them in full, and the lessons carry them.

**A Stage 1 correction, from Unit 5's table.** Stage 1 gave FSI's *w-* (he,
she) and *mw-* (you, pl.) as the past prefixes; Unit 5 labels both
**eastern**. The two lessons now give the central *á-* and *ma-* as the
standard.
